Overview

Rajmudra is a tactics game about ground. Height decides a fight: a blow from above lands harder than one from below, a second man beside the target counts, forest shelters, horse needs room to charge. The maps are built so that a player who reads the ridge wins and a column that climbs loses.

Every board has one objective that can be said in five words, such as Hold the pass ten turns or Raise the flag on Torna. A board is won or lost inside three minutes, and its three stars always mean the same: the objective done, done in good time, and no man lost.

One camera looks down on each board like a model on a table, and cuts only once, to the close-up of a blow. Where the sources disagree, or a story is tradition and not record, the game says so.

The making

Where the idea came from

Rajmudra, named for the royal seal of Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, began on 29 October 2026 as a turn-based tactics game about Shivaji's campaigns, from Torna in 1646 to Raigad in 1674. Its first rule was about truth: nothing is invented beyond the rules of play. The record is the Sabhasad Bakhar, Sarkar's history, the Mughal and Adilshahi chronicles and the Adnyapatra.

The record also decides who falls. Enemy leaders whom the chronicles show captured or released yield on the board and do not die; only Afzal Khan and Udaybhan fall. The look was named in three words, basalt, haze and saffron, and its figures are told apart by outline, dress and colour, not by faces.

The first board, the ambush at Umberkhind, was playable on 31 October 2026.

Story

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj was sixteen when he took the hill fort of Torna in the monsoon of 1646. Twenty-eight years later he sat on the throne at Raigad. The game follows that road, fort by fort, through the passes and plains of the Sahyadri, the mountain wall along the western edge of the Deccan.

The player commands Shivaji and the men the record names beside him: Tanaji Malusare, Baji Prabhu Deshpande, Murarbaji Deshpande, Netaji Palkar, Moropant Pingle and the rest. Each of the fourteen battles is a real place and a real day, from the camp below Pratapgad and the night escape from Panhala to the raid on Shaista Khan's house, the baskets at Agra and the climb at Kondhana.

The enemy are men with reasons. Bijapur, the Mughals and the Siddis send their best commanders, and where the sources record that a leader asked for terms or was let go, the game lets him. Between boards the company gathers in the hall at Rajgad, and a singer of ballads tells what the player has just done.

World

The Sahyadri, also called the Western Ghats, is a wall of black trap rock that runs down the west of the Deccan. Its spurs carry the forts: Torna at 4,605 feet, with Rajgad five miles east on the same crest; Purandar at 4,364 feet, with a ledge round its waist; Salher at 5,295 feet in Baglana.

Below them lie the Maval valleys, the forest of Javli, the black-soil plain of Kolhapur and the road from Nashik to Baglana. Every board is built from the real ground, with the real fort, pass or field named in the chronicles.

Press a map to open it. Drag to move around it; scroll, pinch or use the buttons to zoom.

The campaign, fourteen boards in four acts

  1. Act I the hill forts, 1646 to 1656

    1. Monsoon 1646

      Torna

      Raise the flag on Torna.

      A hill fort at 4,605 feet with Rajgad five miles to the east. In one account about a thousand Mavala foot walk up in the rains while the garrison waits out the weather below; Shivaji is sixteen. The board teaches how to move, climb and strike.

    2. Winter 1655 to 1656

      Javli

      Take Javli from the Mores.

      A forested state in the Koyna valley, walled on the west by the crest of the Sahyadri. The Mores hold it with hill-men who climb as well as the Mavalas do. The board is wider than Torna, with a stream, a ford and a walled compound in a clearing.

  2. Act II Afzal Khan to Shaista Khan, 1659 to 1663

    1. 10 November 1659

      Pratapgad

      Break the camp below Pratapgad.

      Afzal Khan comes down the Radtondi pass with Bijapur's army and camps in scattered groups by the pools at the source of the Koyna. The Marathas wait in the forest on four sides of the valley.

    2. 28 December 1659

      The field below Panhala

      Rout the army below Panhala.

      After Pratapgad the Marathas take Panhala, a fort on uplands about 700 feet above the Kolhapur plain. Bijapur sends an army to the plain below it. An open black-soil field, a dry nala with three fords and a low rise to start from.

    3. 12 to 13 July 1660

      Panhala, at night

      Carry the palanquin past Jauhar.

      Siddi Jauhar holds Panhala in a ring of picket posts through the monsoon. One night Shivaji slips out toward Vishalgad. Rain, torches, vision cones, and the palanquin must stay in the dark between the posts.

    4. 13 July 1660

      The Gajapur pass

      Hold the ravine nine turns.

      Below Vishalgad Baji Prabhu Deshpande holds a narrow ravine with a few men so that the pursuit cannot climb. The board is the first hold: waves of foot and horse climb the track and only so many may get through.

    5. Early 1661

      Umberkhind

      Hold the pass ten turns.

      The track through Umberkhind is likened in one account to the barrel of a gun. Kartalab Khan's column is strung out along it below Lohagad, under ridges held by the Marathas. The column gives up its horses and treasure and is let go.

    6. 5 April 1663

      Shaista Khan's house, Pune

      Reach the Khan's inner room.

      A night raid into the house where Shivaji grew up, now the quarters of the Mughal governor, behind a maze of canvas screens. Torches, vision cones and a single bricked-up door.

  3. Act III Purandar, Agra and the recovery, 1665 to 1670

    1. Spring 1665

      Purandar

      Cut through to Dilir's camp.

      Purandar rises 4,364 feet, with a ledge round its waist where the garrison lives. Murarbaji Deshpande leads out seven hundred men against Dilir Khan's Afghans as they climb the face. The treaty that follows turns twenty-three forts grey on the map.

    2. 19 August 1666

      Agra

      Carry the baskets out unseen.

      Shivaji is held in Agra under guard. No blow is struck on this board: baskets of sweets go out of the house each evening, and the player must get the right ones to the edge of the city.

    3. A dark night, early 1670

      Kondhana

      Get the Kalyan gate open.

      The fort of Kondhana stands about 700 metres above the plain, ringed by black rock. Tanaji Malusare takes the hardest fort of all by the sheer gorge on its west side, at night, with a rope ladder.

  4. Act IV the second war and the coronation, 1670 to 1674

    1. October 1670

      Vani-Dindori

      Cover the baggage eight turns.

      Shivaji comes south from Surat with the plunder and a Mughal army catches him on the plain below the Kanchana-Manchana pass. Horse against guns while the baggage goes home.

    2. January 1672

      Salher

      Break the siege of Salher.

      Two Maratha forces converge on a Mughal siege camp below Salher, a fort at 5,295 feet. The largest field in the game, fought through a cloud of dust.

    3. 6 June 1674

      Raigad

      Escort Shivaji to the throne.

      The last board has no enemy. The company walks the Raje up the single avenue of Raigad, through two narrow gates, to the throne and the coronation. The only test is who is still alive to walk in the line.

The commanders

  • Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj

    Born at Shivneri in 1630. At sixteen he takes Torna; he is crowned at Raigad in 1674. In the game he commands every board and is carried through them, and if he is beaten the map is lost.

  • Jijabai

    His mother. She blessed him before Pratapgad and ruled while he was held at Agra. She dies in June 1674, twelve days after the coronation.

  • Dadoji Kondadeo

    Shahaji's steward at Pune, who kept the boy's accounts and died the winter after Torna.

  • Tanaji Malusare

    A captain at Torna and a Mavala hazari. He asked for Kondhana and took it in 1670.

  • Suryaji Malusare

    Tanaji's brother, who rallied the men at Kondhana and opened the Kalyan gate.

  • Yesaji Kank

    A Mavala chief of Shivaji's own age, a captain at Torna and later over the ten thousand Mavalas.

  • Kanhoji Jedhe

    Deshmukh of Rohidkhore. The Jedhe and Bandal men held the ambush posts at Pratapgad, and his family's papers keep many of the period's firmest dates.

  • Netaji Palkar

    Master of the Horse, called a second Shivaji by the Deccanis.

  • Baji Prabhu Deshpande

    Deshpande of Hardis Maval. He held the Gajapur pass in 1660 so that the pursuit could not climb.

  • Murarbaji Deshpande

    Commander at Purandar in 1665, who took seven hundred men out against Dilir Khan's five thousand.

  • Moropant Pingle

    Moro Trimbak Pingle, who built Pratapgad, led the Konkan army and stood first of the eight ministers at the coronation.

  • Hiroji Farzand

    At Agra he lay on the king's cot with the king's ring on his hand while the king left the house.

  • Prataprao Gujar

    Commander of the horse, who took the siege camp at Salher.

  • Hambirrao Mohite

    A great swordsman, called brave, patient and cautious in the chronicle, and one of the eight ministers at Raigad.

  • Bahirji Naik

    Naik of the spies, picked after great scrutiny, who brought the report on Surat.

The other side

  • Afzal Khan

    Bijapur's commander at Pratapgad in 1659.

  • Fazal Khan

    Afzal Khan's son, who commanded in the field below Panhala.

  • Siddi Jauhar

    Held Panhala in a ring of pickets through the monsoon of 1660.

  • Kartalab Khan

    Mughal commander of the column at Umberkhind.

  • Shaista Khan

    Mughal governor lodged in Pune in 1663.

  • Mirza Raja Jai Singh

    Commanded the Mughal siege of Purandar in 1665.

  • Dilir Khan

    Mughal commander before Purandar.

  • Udaybhan Rathod

    Rajput commander of Kondhana in 1670.

  • Ikhlas Khan

    Mughal commander at Vani-Dindori and Salher.

  • Daud Khan

    Mughal commander at Vani-Dindori.

Reading list

  • Sabhasad Bakhar

    The chronicle of Krishnaji Anant Sabhasad, written in 1694 at the order of Chhatrapati Rajaram. Read in S. N. Sen's translation, Siva Chhatrapati (University of Calcutta, 1920).

  • Shivaji and His Times

    Jadunath Sarkar, second edition (Longmans, 1920).

  • A History of the Maratha People

    C. A. Kincaid and D. B. Parasnis, volume I (1918).

  • History of the Mahrattas

    James Grant Duff, volume I (1826).

  • The Ajnapatra

    Ramchandra Pant Amatya, translated by S. V. Puntambekar, Journal of Indian History VIII (1929).

  • Muntakhab-ul-Lubab

    Khafi Khan, in Elliot and Dowson, The History of India as Told by Its Own Historians, volume VII (1877).

  • Tarikh-i-Dilkasha

    Bhimsen Saxena, translated by Jadunath Sarkar.

  • A New Account of East India and Persia

    John Fryer, with Henry Oxinden's narrative, Hakluyt Society edition.

  • English Records on Shivaji, 1659 to 1682

    Shiva Charitra Karyalaya, 1931.

  • Shivaji: His Life and Times

    G. B. Mehendale (2011).

  • New History of the Marathas

    G. S. Sardesai, volume I (1946).

  • Shivaji the Maratha

    H. G. Rawlinson (1915).

  • Gazetteers of the Bombay Presidency

    Poona, Satara, Kolhapur, Nasik and Ratnagiri volumes, for the forts.
