Overview

Setstones has two hundred levels in ten sets of twenty. You move the marked stones between plinths until each pile holds one kind.

No two levels in a row are quite the same kind of board, and a new rule arrives every few levels rather than once a set. Every level has a gentle board, a set board and a stern one. Solve cleanly and the game deals you the sterner boards. Have a hard patch and it deals the gentler ones, and a board you restart three times settles into an easier one.

Every fifth level is a lantern board: solve it without Undo, or finish one colour first, or move a ringed stone only once. Keep the aim and a lantern is lit and set afloat on the river behind the table.

A solved board is cheered, and the game says something about how you solved it. Each solve earns up to three fireflies, which gather in a jar and over the bank. A flame beside the move count grows with every perfect solve in a row. Twenty-nine awards keep count of what you have done.

Each set has a sky of its own: dusk, afterglow, first stars, moonrise, an ember sky, mist, northern lights, deep night, rain and dawn. Once you have reached a sky you can keep it for every level. There are six pieces of music to choose from: the river's own tune, a slow lofi beat, felt piano, a lazy beat with tabla and flute, a drift of pads and chimes, and harp and strings. Or none at all.

Three levels are open at a time, so one hard board never bars the way. Undo, restart and a hint are always in reach, and there is a daily stone with a count of the days you have kept it up. Leave a board half done and it waits for you: the next time you open the game, Continue puts you back on it, move for move.

The making

Where the idea came from

Setstones began in November 2026 as one sample board and a question: could a sorting puzzle be a quiet place instead of a loud one? Games of this kind are usually tubes of bright balls. We wanted somewhere to be, so the pieces became river stones with marks carved into them, the tubes became plinths on a bank at dusk, and a finished pile became a cairn.

The first people to play it asked for more boards, then for a cheer when a board is solved, and then told us plainly where it grew dull. Each build since has answered one of those: two hundred levels, a river to travel between the sets, lanterns to light, fireflies to gather, and a sky and a tune to choose.

Story

A quiet table at the edge of a lake at dusk. Stones marked with a sun, a wave, a leaf or a spiral sit stacked on plinths in the shallows while fireflies drift past.

There is no timer and no lives. You lift a stone, you set it down, and the pile glows when it is one set. When a level is done the game tells you how many moves you used and what the best possible was.

The river teaches as it goes. A narrow ledge holds only as many stones as it has studs. A carved plinth takes only the colour of its mark. Mist hides every stone but the top one. Later the cairns stand five stones tall, and a sixth and a seventh colour come to the water.

World

The table stands at the edge of a lake at dusk. Hills lie dark across the water, reeds stand in the shallows and fireflies drift past. As you travel the river the sky changes with each set. Everything, the sky, the hills, the river, the plinths and the carved marks, is drawn in the game itself.

The ten sets

First Stones
Levels 1 to 20. Two colours, then three, then four.
Riverbank
Levels 21 to 40. Five colours, and fewer places to rest a stone.
Narrow Ledges
Levels 41 to 60. Ledges of every size, and the first tall cairns.
Carved Plinths
Levels 61 to 80. Carved plinths in twos and threes, and a sixth colour near the end.
Ember
Levels 81 to 100. Six colours, on ledges, on carved plinths and in mist.
River Mist
Levels 101 to 120. Mist over every kind of board.
Tall Cairns
Levels 121 to 140. Five stones to a cairn, and a seventh colour.
Deep Water
Levels 141 to 160. Seven colours, and the bank is crowded.
Crossings
Levels 161 to 180. Ledges, carvings and mist together.
Last Light
Levels 181 to 200. Everything the river has taught you.
